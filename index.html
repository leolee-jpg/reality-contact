The loop every lens shares

The lenses are one system under different names; the abstract lens is that system with no names at all. Each has four parts. The world changes on its own. A shared signal summarizes it. Agents act on the signal. The outcome depends on both the world and what the agents did, and becomes part of the record the next signal can draw on.

The signal can be fed from two directions, and the distinction is the core of the taxonomy. A signal fed by the world is exogenous: it tells agents something they couldn’t see from inside the system. A signal fed by the system’s own past outcomes is endogenous: it tells agents what the system already did. Most real signals are a mix. Reflexivity sets the mix, and reality contact measures how much exogenous information is still getting through.

Why this exists

A good coordination signal compresses what many people know into something everyone can act on. A price tells a buyer about conditions she will never see; a consensus view saves each reader from checking every source; a dashboard lets a leader steer work he can’t watch directly. The signal earns that trust by staying in contact with the world.

The failure that matters is quiet. When participants start acting on the signal itself rather than on what it describes, the signal begins to recirculate the system’s own past. It keeps predicting what happens next, sometimes better than before, because everyone is now coordinating around it. The usual test, whether the signal predicts outcomes, can’t see the problem. It passes right up to the point of failure.

AI speeds this loop up. Agents that trade against prices, models trained on text other models wrote, and reports generated from earlier reports all make it cheaper to act on the signal and costlier to go back to the world. The question this tool asks is narrow on purpose: of what the signal predicts, how much is news the system couldn’t have gotten from its own state? It doesn’t decide what is true. It tells a person when a signal has gone circular, so they know where to look.

Things to try

  • Sweep reflexivity, then lower memory and sweep again. Which kind of recirculation loses contact faster?
  • Find a run where the surface link rises while reality contact falls. What would someone watching only the surface link conclude?
  • Drop to 2,000 steps and rerun a few times. How much of what you saw survives the noise?
  • Raise agent noise. Does a signal that people follow loosely stay more anchored, or just harder to read?

Where it stops

The economy here is a toy with a known answer, built to check that the measure reads reflexivity correctly. Using it on a real system needs records of the system’s state and of what participants did, and signals whose variance hasn’t collapsed. Near equilibrium every measure shrinks at once, and telling a quiet signal from a circular one needs a counterfactual probe: nudge the signal and see how far the expected outcome moves.

What the two numbers are

Surface link is the mutual information between the signal and the next outcome: does the signal predict what happens next?

Reality contact is the same quantity conditioned on what the system already knows, its current state and the actions agents took:

I(ct ; xt+1 | xt, at)

The world changes every step. The signal mixes the current state of the world with a moving average of past outcomes, in the proportion set by reflexivity. Agents act on the signal with some noise, and the next outcome depends on both the world and their actions. Both numbers are estimated in your browser with quantile binning and plug-in entropies, in nats.
